**What is claimed.** That repetition drop-off across sets is a property of the muscle — chest holds
then crashes (12/12/8), back declines gently or holds flat (12/10/10, 10/10/10) — and that
fibre-type composition is the reason, muscles with more type I fibre sustaining repetitions better.
The strong form: a product could hold a per-muscle expected drop-off and read a deviation from it
as a signal about effort or progression.

**What the evidence actually shows.**

*Fibre-type composition by muscle is measured, thinly, and the variance sits in the wrong place for
this claim.* Queeno et al. (2023) pooled 269 studies; the human rows give mean slow-fibre
percentages of soleus 84.8, pectoralis major 55.4, latissimus dorsi 54.4, deltoid 52.1,
gastrocnemius 51.6, biceps brachii 49.5, biceps femoris 47.7, vastus lateralis 46.3, triceps
brachii 31.5.

Two things follow at once. **Outside soleus every trained muscle sits in a band roughly 31-55%
slow — about 24 percentage points wide.** And **pectoralis major and latissimus dorsi are
indistinguishable (55.4 against 54.4), with chest fractionally the *slower*-twitch of the two.**
The specific contrast that motivates the claim is not in the data, and its sign is if anything
backwards. Triceps is the one clear outlier, and it is the most *fast*-twitch muscle listed.

*Within-muscle between-person variance is roughly three times larger.* Simoneau & Bouchard (1989)
typed **418 vastus lateralis biopsies from 418 people**: type I proportions ran from **15% to
85%**, with coefficients of variation near **30%**. Inside one muscle across people the spread is
about 70 percentage points; across muscles at the mean it is about 24. **A per-muscle fibre-type
rule is not coherent as a population rule** — it would assign a difference roughly three times
smaller than the person-level noise it ignores.

The per-muscle means also rest on very little: pectoralis major is **one study, ten people**;
latissimus dorsi **one study, twenty-three**. Johnson et al. (1973) — six cadavers, thirty-six
muscles — remains the origin of most upper-body figures.

*Repetitions-to-failure at a given %1RM do differ by exercise, this is well measured, and fibre
type is not the explanation offered.* Nuzzo et al. (2024) is the definitive source: a
meta-regression of **952 repetitions-to-failure tests from 7,289 individuals across 269 studies**.
At 70% 1RM, leg press yields **19.0** repetitions against bench press **14.1**; at 80%, **13.1**
against **8.8**. That is a large, real difference — but the paper's own conclusion is the important
part: **only bench press and leg press deviated from the general model, and the general model
applies to every other exercise.** Sex, age and training status barely moved it. **The
literature's answer to "do muscles differ" is: two named exercises differ, and everything else is
one curve.** Nuzzo does not discuss fibre type at all. Shimano et al. (2006), the free-weight
version, attributes its difference explicitly to **"the amount of muscle mass used during the
exercise"** — not to phenotype.

*Fibre type has been tested directly as a predictor of repetition performance, three times, and
the results are small and mixed.*

- **Terzis (2008)**, 12 men, vastus lateralis biopsy, leg press at 70% and 85% 1RM: **"a low and
  nonsignificant relationship"** between fibre-type distribution and repetitions. What did
  correlate was **capillary density** (r = 0.70, p = 0.01).
- **Douris (2006)**, untrained women, quadriceps, non-invasive estimate: a **"fair-to-moderate"**
  relationship.
- **Van Vossel (2023)**, the best powered and closest: 95 resistance-training **novices**, typology
  by carnosine MR spectroscopy, 3 sets to failure. Total repetitions related to typology at 80%
  (r = −0.42) and 60% 1RM (r = −0.44) but **not at 40%** — under 20% of the variance explained,
  and the authors' own verdict is that typology "partly determines" the count.

**The design of all three is decisive for this question: fibre type is treated as a property of the
person, not of the muscle.** Van Vossel measured typology in *leg* muscles and used it to predict
*biceps curl* repetitions — the study's whole logic is that typology is a whole-body individual
trait. **Nobody has used the fibre composition of muscle A to predict the fatigue behaviour of
muscle A against muscle B in the same person.** That is the experiment the claim requires and it
does not exist.

*Does drop-off across sets differ by muscle? The one clean same-protocol test is a null.*
Millender et al. (2021), 14 resistance-trained females, chest press and leg press, 4 sets to
failure at 75% of 10RM, 1- against 3-minute rest: a main effect of rest interval on fatigue index
but **not of exercise**, concluding that **"upper and lower body repetition performance was
affected similarly by short RIs, despite differences in muscle size and phenotype."** The authors
raise the phenotype hypothesis and report against it.

Beyond that, set-by-set counts for two exercises under one protocol are almost absent, and what
exists across protocols is not comparable: bench to failure at 70% with 2 min rest gives
12.50 / 6.06 / 4.20 (−66%); at 65% with 2 min, 16.0 / 9.9 / 6.3 (−61%); at 3 RIR with 4 min, a
25-33% fall over six sets (Refalo 2023). **The protocol, not the muscle, dominates that range.**

Ribeiro (2014) remains the one exercise-dependent datum already in this corpus — bench-press total
repetitions +16.3% over 16 weeks against arm curl about 0% — and it does **not** line up with fibre
type: pectoralis (55.4% slow) and biceps (49.5%) are close, and the exercise that adapted is the
one with the larger load, more musculature and more stabiliser involvement.

*Position within the session moves repetitions as much as any muscle effect, and this is well
measured.* Senna et al. (2019), 14 trained men, 5 sets at 10RM load, bench press and machine chest
fly in both orders: bench press yielded **34.36 ± 4.68** total repetitions performed first against
**25.85 ± 6.73** performed second (p = 0.001, **−25%**), with the fly showing the mirror image.
Whole-workout totals were unchanged. **The position effect is the same magnitude as the muscle
effect being claimed**, which makes any log confounding the two uninterpretable. On whether
pre-fatigue *flattens* a sequence rather than lowering it, Senna's text reports both orders showing
"similar and progressive reductions" across the five sets — the level moved and the shape did not
obviously change. That is one sentence in one study, and it points against the flattening
intuition rather than settling it.

*Is there a published per-muscle expected drop-off?* **No.** Nothing found offers a normative
decline per muscle, per exercise or per set index, at any RIR or rest interval. That is an absence,
not a null.

**What it does not settle.**

- **"Muscles differ in fatigue resistance" is supported; "fibre type is why, and it predicts the
  shape of a three-set log" is untested.** Only the first has evidence, and the second has never
  been the design of a study.
- **Whether drop-off *shape* differs by muscle at constant RIR is unmeasured entirely.** Every
  set-by-set dataset found is sets-to-failure except Refalo, which is one exercise. `TD-018`
  prescribes 2 RIR, and nothing describes a three-set sequence at 2 RIR in more than one exercise.
- **Van Vossel's population is novices and its typology was measured in the legs.** Whether the
  correlation survives in trained lifters, and whether a muscle's own composition would beat a
  whole-person estimate, is open in both directions.
- **The per-muscle means are weak enough to move.** Pectoralis rests on ten people. Better data
  showing chest and back genuinely differ would leave the variance argument standing but not the
  sign argument.
- **The null on drop-off by muscle is one study of 14 women.** It is the right design and there is
  not much of it.
- **Whether pre-fatigue flattens or merely lowers a sequence** rests on one sentence of prose.
- **Nothing addresses whether a deviation from a *per-user historical* drop-off carries
  information.** The literature is silent on personal baselines, which is a different question
  from population baselines and the one that might have an answer.

**Provenance caveat.** The per-muscle figures are an extraction of Queeno's supplementary dataset;
the underlying studies were not read, and Johnson (1973)'s own per-muscle values could not be
retrieved from any resolving source. **Anything leaning on the pectoralis-against-latissimus
comparison specifically should verify those two rows against their primary studies first**, because
that comparison is the pivot of this note and rests on one study of ten people against one study of
twenty-three. Van Vossel (2023) is paywalled; everything reported from it is its abstract, and its
set-by-set data — which may exist, since it ran three sets to failure — was not accessible.

**Where it touches the product.**

- **It closes the per-muscle drop-off table.** No such table exists, and the mechanism proposed to
  justify inventing one is contradicted by the variance structure. This is the note to cite when a
  future session proposes per-muscle fatigue expectations — the sibling of
  `references/muscle-group-specific-volume-requirements.md`, and the same ruling.
- **It reinforces `TD-018`.** A uniform proximity across exercises is what a corpus with no
  measured per-muscle fatigue difference supports, and it removes one more candidate reason to
  reinstate a gradient.
- **It sharpens `references/progression-trigger-under-constant-effort-execution.md`.** The expected
  fall **cannot be made muscle-specific**, so a trigger cannot be calibrated per muscle; and
  **session position alone moves an exercise's total by about 25%**, so a trigger reading later
  sets inherits the ordering as a confound.
- **It adds a confound to the flat-sequence flag that
  `references/separating-execution-modes-from-a-bare-log.md` already withdrew** — the same muscle
  trained by three exercises can produce three different drop-offs, so even a per-muscle version of
  that flag would be miscalibrated.
- **If a drop-off baseline is ever built, it must key on the slot and never on the muscle**, and it
  would have to hold four things constant before meaning anything: the exercise, its position in
  the session, the rest actually taken, and the load. Whether that is worth building is a decision
  this note does not make.
- **`TD-009` and `TD-011` are unaffected.** Nothing here argues for muscle-specific repetition
  ranges or rest.
