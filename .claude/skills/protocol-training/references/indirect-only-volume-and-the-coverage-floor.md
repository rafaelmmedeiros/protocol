**What is claimed.** That if an exclusion drops a muscle below its weekly volume floor, that
muscle stops progressing — so the floor is a hard constraint the generator must defend even
against a stated preference.

**What the evidence actually shows.**

*For muscles that are secondaries in compound work, "only indirect volume" is not obviously
worse.* The Schoenfeld/Grgic meta-analysis (7 studies, 10 nested comparisons) reports a
**trivial standardised point estimate** for single-joint versus multi-joint training on
limb-muscle hypertrophy, with no change when stratifying by combined SJ+MJ versus MJ alone, or
by volume-equated versus not. de Franca et al. (2015), 20 men with 2+ years of training, 8
weeks, multi-joint versus multi-joint-plus-single-joint upper body: flexed arm circumference
+1.72% versus +1.45%, no significant between-group difference on any variable. **On this
evidence, a user who excludes every biceps curl but still rows and pulls down has not starved
their biceps in any way the literature can detect.**

*Against that, the one within-subject trial isolating the contrast disagrees sharply.* Mannarino
et al. (2021): 10 untrained men, one arm dumbbell row, the other dumbbell curl, 8 weeks,
matched sets to failure. Elbow flexor thickness **+11.06% curl versus +5.16% row** — a factor of
two. It is small and untrained, and it is the study most directly built to answer the question,
and it points the opposite way to the pooled estimate. **That is why this note is `contested`.**

*For muscles no compound covers, none of the above applies, and the arithmetic is unforgiving.*
The rear deltoid, the lateral deltoid and the calves have no multi-joint exercise for which they
are the prime mover in a typical catalogue. Pelland's meta-regression fits weekly volume with a
**square root** — monotonic, concave, no plateau — which means the marginal value of a set is
*highest at the bottom of the range*. **Sets removed from a muscle at 4.0 are the most expensive
sets in the programme; sets removed from a muscle at 10.0 are the cheapest.** `TD-008`'s floor is
not an arbitrary line: it is where the curve is steepest.

*But below-floor is not zero, and maintenance is far cheaper than growth.* Bickel et al. (2011)
took trained-up adults and cut them from 3 sessions a week of 3 sets to 1 session of 1 set —
roughly **one ninth of the prior volume** — for 32 weeks. In the young group muscle size was
**fully maintained** and 1RM strength continued to improve; in the older group size was not. So a
muscle that falls below the floor for a block is losing *progress*, not tissue, provided some
stimulus remains and the user is not elderly.

*Regional adaptation is the residue none of this covers.* Chaves et al. (2020) found incline
pressing produced 0.62 cm more upper-pectoralis thickness than flat (p=0.003) with nothing at two
other sites. An exclusion that removes the only exercise loading a region may cost something no
whole-muscle set count can see.

**What it does not settle.**

- **The direct question — what happens when a *user-chosen* exclusion drops a muscle below a
  floor — has never been studied**, and plausibly never will be. Everything above is assembled
  from adjacent contrasts.
- **The pooled null and the strongest single trial disagree**, and sample quality does not
  resolve it: Mannarino is n=10 untrained but within-subject and purpose-built; the meta pools 7
  heterogeneous studies. Do not treat "indirect volume is equivalent" as established.
- **`TD-006`'s 0.5 weighting is not validated by any of this.** These trials compare the
  *presence* of isolation work, not a fractional accounting of it. If Mannarino is right, 0.5
  flatters indirect work; if the meta is right, 0.5 penalises it. The constant remains a
  convention.
- **Bickel is a maintenance phase after 16 weeks of training, in people who had already gained.**
  It says nothing about a novice whose muscle never got above the floor — which is exactly the
  cold-start case.
- **No evidence prices a shortfall.** "3.2 sets instead of 6.0 for eight weeks" cannot be
  converted into millimetres by anything here.
- **Nothing establishes a fraction-of-catalogue threshold.** There is no study, in any field, of
  how much of an exercise menu a person can remove before the rest is inadequate.

**Provenance caveat.** The Schoenfeld/Grgic meta-analysis and de Franca's percentages reached
this note through search summaries — the publishers returned 402 and 403 — so the direction and
"trivial point estimate" are attested but **the numeric effect size and its interval are not**.
Verify before any decision turns on the magnitude.

**Where it touches the product.**

- It is the reason a coverage failure is **reported per muscle, with the number**, rather than as
  a verdict on the programme. The evidence supports "rear delts reach 2.0 of 6.0 this week"; it
  does not support "your programme is inadequate".
- **It splits the catalogue into two classes for exclusion purposes:** muscles reachable through
  compounds, where an exclusion is cheap and contested, and muscles reachable only through direct
  slots — side delts, rear delts, calves — where it is expensive and uncontested. `TD-004`'s
  `knee_flexion` hole is already an instance of the second class, arrived at from the catalogue
  side rather than the user side.
- It supports `TD-008`'s floor of 4.0 as *the place the curve is steepest*, which is a better
  justification than the one originally recorded, and it does not move the number.
- It argues against auto-relaxing the floor when an exclusion bites: the shortfall is real,
  concave-curve-expensive, and worth surfacing (`TD-016`).
