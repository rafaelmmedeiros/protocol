**What is claimed.** That resistance training requires a warm-up — general cardiovascular work
plus progressive ramping sets on each exercise — and that this is a fixed, non-negotiable cost in
a session's time budget.

**What the evidence actually shows.** Weakly supported at heavy loads, unsupported at moderate
loads, and much cheaper than convention assumes.

*General warm-up is the first thing the literature discards.* Iversen et al.'s narrative review on
time-efficient training recommends restricting warm-up to exercise-specific work and
deprioritising stretching unless flexibility is itself the goal, citing evidence that ten minutes
of cycling produced only trivial changes in peak power against no warm-up. This is the review's
clearest time-efficiency recommendation and the one with least dissent.

*Specific warm-up at moderate loads: negligible.* The strongest recent test is a 2025 crossover in
**29 trained lifters** (4.5 +/- 3.9 years experience) comparing one warm-up set (3-4 reps at 75%
of 10RM), two warm-up sets (55% then 75%), and **no warm-up at all**, before four working sets to
concentric failure at 10RM on bench press and leg press. Differences across every outcome —
repetitions, fatigue index, volume load, readiness, RPE — were **negligible to small**, and the
authors conclude that time efficiency can be gained by forgoing the specific warm-up at ~10RM
loads. Ribeiro et al. (2014) reached the same null earlier in 15 men at 80% 1RM to failure: no
difference in summed repetitions or fatigue index across warm-up conditions.

*At heavier loads there is a small, real effect, and it is about velocity rather than volume.* A
crossover in 40 resistance-trained men at 80% 1RM found a progressive two-set warm-up (6 reps at
40%, then 6 at 80% of the training load) produced marginally higher total work in bench press
(4750 +/- 1313 vs 4632 +/- 1355 J, p = 0.01) and that a single heavy set improved mean propulsive
velocity in the squat (0.69 vs 0.67 m/s, p = 0.02). These are ~2% effects on mechanical output,
not on repetitions completed.

*The time cost, computed.* Two ramping sets of 3-6 light repetitions with a short rest between
them is roughly **2.5-3 minutes** including the approach to the first working set. Against the
model in `references/session-time-cost-of-a-set.md`, that is under 5% of a 60-minute session —
but it is 10% of a 30-minute one, and it is the difference between fitting a fourth exercise and
not.

*Position stands are silent.* ACSM's 2026 overview of reviews, built on 137 systematic reviews,
makes no warm-up recommendation at all. Its absence from a document of that scope is itself
informative about how much evidence exists.

**What it does not settle.**

- **Every trial measured performance; none measured injury.** The entire case for warming up as
  injury prophylaxis is unexamined here, and "no effect on repetitions" is not "no effect on
  risk". This is the boundary most likely to be crossed by someone reading the performance nulls
  as a licence to omit warm-up entirely.
- **Populations are trained young adults.** Nothing addresses older lifters, cold environments,
  early-morning sessions, or a first exposure to an unfamiliar movement — where the argument for
  ramping is about motor learning and load calibration rather than potentiation. That last case
  is exactly the cold-start user (`TD-001`).
- **Ramping doubles as load-finding.** A lifter who does not know their working weight needs
  ramping sets to discover it, whatever the physiology says. No trial isolates that function,
  because trials pre-test 1RM. Our users have no prescribed load at all in `M1`.
- **Nothing tests warm-up on the second, third and fourth exercise of a session.** The intuition
  that a later exercise inherits the first one's warm-up is universal practitioner convention and
  entirely untested.
- **The heavier-load benefit was measured against zero warm-up**, not against a shorter one. It
  does not establish how many ramping sets are optimal; the practitioner range of 2-5 has no trial
  behind it.

**Where it touches the product.** The fixed overhead term in the session time budget (`TD-012`),
and whether warm-up sets are generated as prescription rows at all. It is also the cheapest thing
to cut when time is short (`TD-013`) — and the cheapness is evidenced, which is unusual for
anything a time budget wants to remove.
