**What is claimed.** That grip determines whether a curl trains the forearm: a neutral or pronated
grip loads the brachioradialis, a supinated one hands the work to the biceps. It is the practitioner
belief behind calling a hammer curl a "forearm exercise" and a bicep curl not.

**What the evidence actually shows.** The opposite direction as often as this one, and never an
on/off switch.

**Caufriez 2018** (n=15, healthy males, isokinetic elbow flexion at 60°/s through a 120° arc,
surface EMG of biceps, brachialis and brachioradialis in full pronation, neutral and full
supination) is the cleanest test of the claim, because it varies only forearm rotation. All three
muscles were "electromyographically very active throughout resisted elbow flexion, **in all three
investigated positions of forearm rotation**". For the brachioradialis specifically, activity was
**slightly higher in supination**. Its conclusion names the brachioradialis one of the main elbow
flexors "especially when the elbow flexion is done with the forearm in supination."

**Uysal 2026** (n=12 resistance-trained males, barbell curl, supinated / semi-supinated / neutral,
sEMG normalised to MVIC, Bayesian analysis) tested the same question in the actual exercise.
Brachioradialis activation **exceeded** biceps activation during eccentric actions "particularly in
the **supinated** and semi-supinated grip-bar configurations". Again the supinated grip is where the
forearm works hardest, not least.

**Chen 2025** (n=36, isometric, 1 kg, 15 combinations of three forearm rotations by five elbow
angles) is the one that points the other way, and only partly. Brachioradialis RMS was *lower* in
supination at 30-60° but pronation was higher only at 90-120°; its summary sentence — "BB appearing
more active in supination and BR in pronation" — is an angle-dependent tendency across a range, not
a threshold. And it is a 1 kg isometric task, which is not a set of curls.

The three disagree about direction. They agree about magnitude: the brachioradialis is substantially
active under every grip tested, in every study.

**What it does not settle.**

- **All three measure acute activation, not growth.** This is the boundary that matters most here.
  `exercise-variant-and-implementation` already holds that within-movement variants are null for
  hypertrophy in four of five trials, so an EMG difference is not evidence of a different training
  effect even when it is real and reproducible. Anything built on "grip changes activation,
  therefore grip changes the muscle trained" is using EMG as a stand-in for hypertrophy, which this
  corpus rejects elsewhere and should reject here.
- **Sample sizes are 12, 15 and 36**, all male, all acute, two of them not performing a training set
  at all. None was designed to rank exercises for a program.
- **Nothing here addresses the wrist.** Wrist flexion and extension are a different joint action
  from elbow flexion, and no study above loaded them. That the forearm is trained in every curl says
  nothing about whether a wrist curl trains it *more directly* — that is anatomy, not this evidence.
- **Brachialis is barely discussed** in the practitioner version of this claim and is a major elbow
  flexor in all three studies. If grip mattered the way the belief says, brachialis is where the
  argument would have to be made.

**Where it touches the product.** It closed a question rather than opening one. `M4` proposed
narrowing the muscle attribution of supinated curls — dropping `Forearms` as a secondary — so that a
hammer curl and a reverse curl would differ from a bicep curl in the model and could earn their own
catalogue rows. The premise was that a supinated curl does not meaningfully load the forearm.
**That premise is contradicted**, most directly by the only study that varies nothing but grip.
`M1`'s attribution — `Forearms` secondary on every curl — is what the evidence supports, and it
stays.
