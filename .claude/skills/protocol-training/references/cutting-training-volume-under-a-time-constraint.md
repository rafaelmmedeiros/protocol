**What is claimed.** That when a programme does not fit the time available, there is a right order
in which to give things up — and specifically that rest, sets, exercises, repetitions and training
days are not interchangeable currencies.

**What the evidence actually shows.** Each candidate lever has been studied in isolation. Their
relative ranking has not. What follows is the levers with their measured costs, and then the
inference that orders them.

*The lever with the most time in it is rest, and it is the one the evidence says costs least.*
Rest is 74-79% of measured session duration (`references/session-time-cost-of-a-set.md`). Pooled
hypertrophy comparisons of short versus long rest cross zero in every region; what short rest
costs is repetitions, and the acute literature places nearly the whole repetition penalty in the
step below two minutes, not between two and three. This is the basis for cutting rest first — and
also the basis for a floor, since the same evidence argues against one-minute intervals
(`TD-011`).

*Restructuring density buys time at no measured volume cost.* A 2025 meta-analysis of **19
studies, 313 participants** found supersets produced statistically indistinguishable chronic
adaptations against traditional sets — hypertrophy SMD **-0.05** (p = 0.87), 1RM SMD 0.10
(p = 0.36), strength endurance SMD 0.07 — while reducing session duration by **~37%**. Drop sets
show the same pattern: hypertrophy SMD 0.555 vs 0.437 for traditional with **no significant
between-group difference**, at **54-70%** less exercise duration. Iversen et al. summarise
supersets, drop sets and rest-pause as roughly halving session time at maintained volume, citing a
rest-pause trial cutting a session from **57 to 35 minutes**. The measured cost is perceptual and
metabolic: supersets raise RPE, blood lactate and internal load. In the affective-response trial
the superset session ran **35 +/- 3 min vs 58 +/- 3 min** traditional, and 18 of 29 participants
preferred it — but rated it 1.3 RPE points harder and 1.0 points more uncomfortable.

*Cutting volume is the lever with a known price, and concavity says spread the cut.* Pelland's
best-fitting dose-response is a square root: monotonic, continuously diminishing, no plateau.
Concavity has a direct consequence for distributing a fixed shortfall. Removing two sets from one
muscle rather than one from each of two, both starting at 8, gives sqrt(6) + sqrt(8) = 5.277
against sqrt(7) + sqrt(7) = 5.292 — spreading wins, but by 0.3%. **The argument only becomes
substantial when concentrating the cut takes a muscle toward zero**, where the curve is steepest:
dropping an exercise that is a muscle's *only* direct work is a move from 3 sets to 0, in the
region where sets are worth most.

*A very small dose is still worth prescribing.* Androulakis-Korakakis et al. found a single set of
6-12 repetitions at 70-85% 1RM, 2-3 times weekly for 8-12 weeks, produced statistically
significant if suboptimal 1RM gains in trained men. Iversen et al. put the practical minimum at
**four weekly sets per muscle group** in a 6-15RM range, and the minimum viable session structure
at three exercises: one leg press pattern, one upper-body pull, one upper-body push. **There is no
duration below which training is worthless; there is a structure below which a session stops
covering the body.**

*Repetition range is a weak time lever and a confounded one.* Fewer repetitions per set is faster
— 8 reps at 3 s is 24 s against 38 s for 12-13 reps — but heavier sets are exactly the ones
conventionally given longer rest, and rest dominates. At constant rest the saving is ~10% of set
time, which is ~2% of a session. Moving toward higher repetitions with shorter rest is the
time-efficient direction, but it inherits the short-rest repetition penalty and, critically, the
"repetition range is free for growth" finding is conditional on sets being near failure — which
`TD-010` does not satisfy.

*Frequency is not the product's lever.* Splitting a session in two is physiologically neutral — 23
trained women completing six lower-body exercises as one 46-minute session or two sessions
totalling 43 minutes differed by 3% in repetitions completed — but **22 of 23 preferred the single
long session**. And a cohort of **522,994** app users found longer workout duration associated
with *better* adherence among users training frequently, with consistency in the first 28 days the
strongest predictor overall. **The intuition that shorter sessions protect adherence is not
supported; if anything the association runs the other way.**

**What it does not settle.**

- **No trial has compared cut strategies against each other.** Nobody has randomised
  time-constrained lifters to "shorter rest, same sets" versus "same rest, fewer sets" and
  measured hypertrophy. The ordering is inference from separate literatures, and it is the whole
  content of this note. Grade accordingly.
- **The density evidence is thinner than the pooled effect sizes suggest.** Only **3 of 19**
  studies in the superset meta-analysis examined chronic outcomes; the rest are acute. A near-zero
  SMD computed largely from acute mechanics is not a demonstration of equal long-term hypertrophy.
- **The floor below which a session should be refused is not an evidential quantity.** Evidence
  supports a minimum weekly volume per muscle and a minimum session structure; converting those
  into minutes requires the time model, which is arithmetic. Any refusal threshold is a product
  bound, in the sense that `TD-002` rejecting seven days a week is a product bound.
- **The concavity argument for spreading a cut is real and tiny** at plausible volumes. It should
  not be cited as though it were large; the strong version is only about taking a muscle to zero.
- **Higher-RPE density techniques and adherence.** Both meta-analyses flag elevated perceived
  exertion, and the adherence literature treats early experience as the predictor of retention.
  Nobody has connected the two. A time-saving technique that makes the first month feel harder is
  an untested bet, and `references/cold-start-first-block.md` is exactly why that matters.
- **All of it is young trained adults over 6-12 weeks.** Nothing addresses a cut ordering
  sustained across months.

**Where it touches the product.** Everything a time-constrained generator does when the
prescription does not fit: which parameter it relaxes first, whether it may restructure into
paired sets, whether it reduces sets per exercise before removing an exercise, and at what point
it refuses to generate rather than emit a session that silently under-delivers. Recorded as
`TD-013`.
