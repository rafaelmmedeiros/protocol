bearing: No trial has compared a graded-RIR prescription against a uniform one, and that null is expected to persist. What changed is the rationale: RIR accuracy does not vary by exercise type and is *better* under heavy load, so the gradient's accuracy argument runs backwards, and local fatigue after an isolation exercise can exceed that after a compound. ACSM 2026 prescribes one exercise-agnostic 2-3 RIR target. The gradient is now unevidenced *and* weakly argued.

**What is claimed.** That proximity to failure should be prescribed differently by exercise type —
further from failure on heavy multi-joint compounds, closer on isolation work — because misjudging
RIR under a loaded bar is both more likely and more costly, and because compound movements carry a
larger fatigue cost. The competing claim is one proximity target for every exercise.

**What the evidence actually shows.**

*The head-to-head comparison still does not exist.* Searching to August 2026 found no trial, in any
population, randomising a graded-RIR programme against a uniform-RIR one. `TD-010`'s statement was
correct when written and remains correct. Every proximity trial compares failure against some
non-failure target; where a trial uses two different targets it uses them on **different exercises
within the same arm**, which confounds rather than tests — Refalo et al. prescribe leg press at 2
RIR and leg extension at 1 RIR in the same condition, with no stated rationale. **The gradient is
embedded in study designs as an unexamined convention.** This null is the expected finding, not an
artefact of searching badly: the trial that would settle it is expensive and low-priority.

*RIR accuracy does not vary by exercise type, and load moves it the wrong way for the gradient.*
This is the sub-claim with real evidence, and it runs against the gradient.

- Halperin et al. (2022): 12 studies, **414 participants, 262 effect sizes**. Mean underprediction
  **0.95 repetitions** — people had about one more repetition than they thought. Accuracy improved
  **closer to failure**, in **later sets**, and with **heavier loads**, with no significant
  difference once repetitions per set fell below roughly 12. **Upper versus lower body did not
  moderate. Training experience did not moderate.** Heterogeneity was extreme (I² = 97.9%).
- Remmert, Laurson & Zourdos (2023): 58 participants, trained and untrained, biceps curl / triceps
  pushdown / seated row at 72.5% 1RM to failure. **No significant main effect of exercise** on RIR
  accuracy; sex, experience and prior RIR-rating experience also null.

Together: the heavy low-rep primary compound is where RIR judgement is *most* accurate, and the
light high-rep isolation slot — `TD-009`'s 10-15 — is on the wrong side of Halperin's ~12-repetition
boundary. **The accuracy argument as `TD-010` states it is inverted by the best evidence on it.** A
gradient justified on accuracy alone would run the other way.

One finding points the other way, and it is a *bias* rather than an accuracy loss: a 2025 six-week
study of 19 well-trained lifters (2,972 velocity/perceived-RIR pairs, squat and bench at 77-89%
1RM) found every participant reported a lower perceived RIR for a given bar velocity in the squat
than in the bench (β = -0.67, p < 0.001) — lifters feel closer to failure in the squat than they
mechanically are.

*Fatigue does differ by exercise type — in both directions, and nobody converts it into proximity
advice.* At the session level, a crossover in 10 trained men found multi-joint and single-joint
sessions produced similar lactate and growth hormone responses but significantly higher creatine
kinase after the multi-joint session, still elevated at 36 h. **At the muscle level the result
inverts:** Soares et al. (2015), 16 highly trained men, contralateral arms, 8x10RM unilateral
seated row against 8x10RM unilateral preacher curl — elbow-flexor isometric peak torque fell
**26.8% after the single-joint exercise against 15.1% after the multi-joint**, and at 24 h the
flexors remained 8.4% below baseline after the isolation exercise while the compound had recovered.
For the muscle actually being trained, the isolation exercise was more fatiguing and slower to
recover. **Neither result was converted by its authors into a proximity recommendation**; both are
framed as recovery-window findings, which are arguments about frequency and volume.

*Prescription complexity and adherence: nothing in resistance training.* The only directly relevant
primary source found is Henry et al. (1999) — adults over 65 given 2, 5 or 8 home exercises, with
better compliance and movement quality at fewer. Different population, different intervention, and
a count of *exercises* rather than of *parameter values*. **Whether a multi-valued effort
prescription is followed less faithfully than a single-valued one is unstudied.**

*What is actually prescribed.* ACSM 2026, an overview of 137 reviews, gives a **single,
exercise-agnostic target**: sufficient effort is accomplished by "completion of 'near-failure' or a
target of 2-3 repetitions in reserve", with momentary failure unnecessary. It makes no
compound-versus-isolation distinction anywhere in its proximity guidance. The proximity
meta-analyses report a continuous RIR dose-response; none stratifies by exercise type and none
recommends stratifying. **The graded prescription appears in coaching writing and silently in trial
designs, and in no position stand or review recommendation located here.**

**What it does not settle.**

- **Whether the gradient helps or hurts.** The absence of a comparison is an absence, not a null
  result. Uniform prescription wins on parsimony and on the collapse of the gradient's rationale,
  never on outcome data.
- **The comparison the gradient's claim is actually about.** Remmert used machine and cable
  exercises exclusively; Halperin's upper/lower null aggregates heterogeneous protocols at
  I² = 97.9%. **Nobody has tested RIR accuracy on a barbell squat against a machine isolation
  exercise in the same subjects at the same proximity** — which is precisely the comparison the
  gradient asserts.
- **Injury and technical breakdown.** No source tests injury rate or technical failure by proximity
  to failure. The safety half of the compound argument is unmeasured, and it is the half on which a
  gradient could still be defended.
- **Which fatigue matters.** Soares and Nunes disagree about which exercise type is "more
  fatiguing" because they measure different things, and neither measures a hypertrophy outcome.
- **The least-evidenced cell remains least evidenced:** a 10-15 repetition set at 2 RIR is both
  `TD-009`'s weakest combination and, per Halperin, where RIR judgement is worst.

**Provenance caveat.** Two figures could not be verified at source. **Halperin's moderator table**
was read from the abstract via two independent mirrors — the pooled 0.95-repetition
underprediction, the sample, and the moderator *directions* are reported there, but the Springer
full text is paywalled and the per-moderator numbers for upper-versus-lower body were never seen,
only the statement of non-significance. **Soares's 26.8% / 15.1%** came from a search snippet of an
abstract that could not be fetched directly; the direction is corroborated by the paper's title and
a second study in the same line, but treat the exact percentages as unverified. A decision resting
on "exercise type does not affect accuracy" deserves one look at Halperin's full text.

**Where it touches the product.**

- **It is why `TD-018` supersedes `TD-010`.** `TD-010` already graded its gradient as convention;
  this note weakens two of the three reasons given for it. Only "discomfort is higher on a heavy
  compound" survives, and Refalo 2025 measures discomfort as a function of *proximity*, not of
  exercise type.
- **A uniform target is what the corpus can defend by citation.** A gradient may still be chosen,
  but it would now be chosen *against* a position stand rather than in the absence of one, and the
  record would have to say so.
- **It does not touch `TD-009`.** Rep ranges rest on load and joint demand. The coupling `TD-009`
  states — that its ceiling exists because the RIR target is conservative — is unaffected, because
  the isolation slot's RIR does not move.
- **It does not touch `TD-017`.** How many distinct RIR values the generator emits has no bearing
  on the conversion at the boundary.
- **An inference worth *not* making.** That the squat's perceptual bias makes a prescribed gradient
  redundant is a plausible reading of the 2025 velocity study and **not** what its authors claim;
  they say only that perceived RIR should be interpreted in the context of exercise type. No record
  should cite it as a finding.
