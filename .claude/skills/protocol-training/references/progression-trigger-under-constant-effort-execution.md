**What is claimed.** That "double progression" is a well-defined rule — hold load, accumulate
repetitions inside a range, add load when the top of the range is reached — and that it can be
implemented directly from a logged session. And behind it, that a repetition count means the same
thing on set 1 and on set 3.

**What the evidence actually shows.**

*The rule is not well defined, and the ambiguity is in the sources.* ACSM's 2009 progression stand
says a **"2-10% increase in load be applied when the individual can perform the current workload
for one to two repetitions over the desired number."** It names **no set**, and is silent on
whether "the current workload" is a set, an exercise or a session, and on whether the surplus must
recur. **That silence is the finding.**

The NSCA's textbook rule is not silent and answers differently. The **2-for-2 rule**: two or more
repetitions over the goal, **in the last set**, on **two consecutive workouts**, then add load. So
the two most-cited progression conventions in the field disagree about which set the rule reads,
and **neither is derived from a trial.**

*Holding proximity to failure constant does not hold repetitions constant, and the magnitude is
large.* Refalo et al. (2023) is the direct measurement: 24 resistance-trained participants
(minimum three years), barbell bench press at **75% 1RM, six sets, four minutes' rest**, under
failure, 1-RIR and 3-RIR conditions.

| Condition | Males, set 1 → final | Females, set 1 → final |
|---|---|---|
| 3 RIR | 9 ± 2 → 6 ± 2 (**−33%**) | 12 ± 3 → 9 ± 2 (−25%) |
| 1 RIR | 11 ± 2 → 6 ± 1 (−45%) | 14 ± 3 → 9 ± 2 (−36%) |
| Failure | 12 ± 2 → 5 ± 1 (−58%) | 16 ± 3 → 8 ± 2 (−50%) |

**A falling sequence is the expected output of constant-effort execution, not evidence that
anything went wrong.** Mean lifting velocity from first to final set fell only 8.1% (M) and 1.1%
(F) at 3 RIR against 28.8% and 19.6% at failure — at a constant moderate RIR the *velocity* is
largely preserved while the *repetitions* fall by a third, which is the mechanical signature of
trading repetitions to hold effort.

*The rest intervals `TD-011` prescribes make this steeper, and nobody has measured it.* Refalo
allowed **four minutes**; `TD-011` prescribes 180 / 150 / 90 s. Every rest study measuring
repetition sustainability does so with sets taken **to failure** — Willardson & Burkett found
significant declines at 30 s, 1 min and 2 min. **No study crosses rest interval with fixed-RIR
execution.** The direction is safe (less rest, steeper drop); the magnitude at our intervals is
unmeasured, and the isolation slot at 90 s is nearest the penalised region.

*"Constant RIR, variable reps" is a recognised mode, but the literature is mostly the other
flavour.* Two things travel under "autoregulation":

- **Load autoregulation** — reps fixed, load moves to hit a target RPE. Helms 2018 and Graham &
  Cleather 2021 both favour it, on **1RM**. Hickmott's meta-analysis pools it to a null
  (MD 2.07 kg, 95% CI −0.32 to 4.46) with **no hypertrophy advantage**.
- **Repetition autoregulation** — load fixed, the set terminates at a target RIR, reps vary. This
  is the mode in question, and **no trial compares it against a fixed repetition target for
  hypertrophy.** Plotkin 2022 is the nearest thing in this corpus and is not it: its REPS arm
  progressed repetitions to momentary failure.

*Terminating on perceived velocity loss is anchored to the right construct and measures it
loosely.* Zourdos 2016 validated the RIR-based scale against mean concentric velocity — r = −0.88
experienced, −0.77 novice — so "stop when the bar slows" is the construct the scale was built on
rather than a folk substitute. The field version is far noisier: PeerJ (2025), 19 well-trained
lifters, **2,972 velocity/perceived-RIR pairs**, individual correlations from trivial to very
large with an **average r² of about 0.3**, and exercise, load, threshold and **set number** all
shifting the relationship — about **−0.1 RIR per set**, meaning later sets are reported slightly
*closer* to failure than velocity says.

*Which set carries the most information: nothing is validated.* No study compares first-set reps,
total reps or volume load as a progression trigger against an outcome. What exists:

- **APRE** (Mann 2010) is the only set-specific trigger with a trial: set 3 is an AMRAP whose rep
  count maps through a table to the next load. It **requires a set to failure**, which `TD-010`
  forbids; the outcome is strength; and it confounds the trigger with the whole programme.
- **2-for-2** reads the last set. No trial.
- **The RM-zone rule** (Scarpelli 2023, in `progression-and-periodization-for-hypertrophy`) is the
  only progression-rule comparison with a hypertrophy outcome — and it is 24 untrained men.
- **Volume load** is a monitoring metric, not an established trigger, and this corpus already holds
  that volume load and hypertrophy dissociate.

*The first-set conflict is real and does not resolve.* The first set is the least
fatigue-confounded *performance* measurement — the only one whose rep count reflects the load
rather than the load plus the order. But Hackett 2018 (in this corpus) found the RIR *estimate* is
worst on the first set of an exercise, 2.0 repetitions of error falling to 0.6 by the third. These
are different quantities and both stand. **A trigger reading the first set gets the cleanest
number attached to the least trustworthy effort label**, and the more the rule assumes effort was
constant, the more that costs. Nothing found tests a first-set trigger against anything.

*Fatigue resistance does improve over a block, measured once, in untrained people.* Ribeiro et al.
(2014), 58 men and 65 women, previously untrained, 16 weeks: bench press total repetitions across
four sets rose **18.4 → 21.4 (+16.3%) in men**, and the fatigue index fell from 74.2% to 65.7%.
**Arm curl total repetitions barely moved** (+2.6% men, −2.5% women) though its fatigue index still
improved. Flattening is a real trained adaptation, it is **exercise-dependent**, and it is
separable from the set-1 count.

**What it does not settle.**

- **Which set the trigger should read.** ACSM does not say; NSCA says the last; APRE tested the
  third. None has been compared against another, for any outcome. **Whatever the product picks is
  a bet, and the options are not equivalent** — under constant-RIR execution a last-set or
  all-sets reading of "top of the range" approaches never firing.
- **Whether crossing the top of a range is the right trigger at all under this execution.** Under
  a fixed rep target the reps are censored at the target, so exceeding it is a discrete event
  carrying information. Under self-terminated effort the reps are an uncensored, noisy,
  fatigue-ordered measurement, and a range boundary is an arbitrary threshold on a continuous
  signal. **That is reasoning, not a finding.**
- **The drop-off at three sets and at 90-180 s rest.** Refalo reports only sets 1 and 6 of six, at
  four minutes. The set-1-to-set-3 figure — the one the product actually needs — is not reported
  by anyone found, and interpolating it would be inventing a number.
- **Whether drop-off *shape* verifies effort.** Nothing found uses a flat sequence to detect
  submaximal early sets, in either direction. Refalo adds **mechanistic support for the asymmetry,
  not validation of it**: since even 3-RIR sets at generous rest fell by a quarter to a third, a
  genuinely flat sequence at fixed load and shorter rest is hard to produce at constant
  near-failure effort. **The grade in
  `references/inferring-proximity-to-failure-from-logged-sets.md` stays `thin`** — a
  better-motivated prior, still confounded by load selection, by rest actually taken, and by a
  lifter who simply stops at a prescribed number.
- **What a flattening sequence means.** 12/10/8 becoming 12/12/11 is produced equally well by
  improved fatigue resistance, by drifting further from failure on the early sets, by improved RIR
  estimation with practice, or by better pacing. **Flattening is not identified**, and nobody asks
  whether it predicts anything.
- **Everything positive about autoregulation is a strength result.** Hickmott's pooled result is a
  null and reports no hypertrophy advantage.
- **Every figure here is bench press, squat or arm curl in a lab.** Nothing addresses a
  self-coached lifter reading their own log months in.

**Provenance caveat.** Four figures rest on secondary sources. **The ACSM sentence** is reported
from multiple secondary sources agreeing; one snippet attached "on two consecutive training
sessions" to ACSM, and that clause is definitely in the NSCA rule — **this note deliberately does
not attribute it to ACSM**, and anything leaning on a recurrence clause must verify it first.
**The 2-for-2 wording**, whose "last set" clause is the pivot of the whole comparison, comes from
certification summaries agreeing with each other rather than from Baechle & Earle directly.
**Refalo's set-by-set table** was retrievable only for sets 1 and 6. **Graham & Cleather's actual
rule** — which set it reads, against what criterion — could not be extracted; only the outcome
percentages. A record citing that trial as precedent for a specific rule must read it first.

**Where it touches the product.**

- **It is the input to the record that decides the load-progression trigger, and the decision
  cannot be deferred to the literature, because the literature does not contain it.**
- **It falsifies the naive rule before it ships.** "Increase load once the top of the range is
  reached", read across all sets or on the last set, silently never fires for a lifter executing at
  constant RIR. A record choosing a set must name this failure mode explicitly, because a future
  session will find the 2-for-2 rule and think it is fixing a bug.
- **The candidate rules, honestly graded.** First set exceeds the range top: convention plus
  reasoning, no trial, weakest where Hackett says the effort label is least reliable. Same with a
  two-session recurrence clause: adds a free noise filter, itself untested. Last set (2-for-2):
  explicit convention, and **contraindicated here** — this is the silent-failure rule. All sets:
  rests on nothing and is strictly worse. Total repetitions across sets: reasoning; robust to which
  set the reps land in, threshold invented, pairs badly with variable set counts. Volume load:
  convention, weakly contraindicated by the dissociation already in this corpus. Set-3 AMRAP
  (APRE): tested and unusable, since `TD-010` forbids failure. Load autoregulation: contested,
  strength-only, and it destroys the drop-off signal while needing an increment finer than
  `load-increment-granularity-and-progression` says exists on isolation work. Velocity: tested,
  hardware-bound, already ruled out.
- **It changes what `TD-009`'s rep range *means* under this execution mode.** A range is a target
  under fixed-rep execution and a **description of where sets are expected to land** under
  terminate-on-effort execution. If the product intends the latter, a record should say so.
- **It touches `TD-010`.** A trigger assuming constant RIR inherits that record's largest stated
  gap — prescribed RIR is not measured RIR — and the r² of 0.3 quantifies how loose the lifter's
  own claim of constancy is even when sincere.
- **It touches `TD-011`.** Cutting rest toward the 90 s floor steepens the drop-off and shifts any
  trigger reading later sets. **A trigger keyed to set 1 is the one least sensitive to the rest
  prescription** — which is an argument for it that has nothing to do with fatigue confounding.
- **It does not license a stall detector or a deload.**
