bearing: Letting the user exclude exercises is cheap and probably slightly positive, but the chain "choice to adherence to growth" has no direct test in resistance training. The one meta-analytic link that survives is autonomy-supportive intervention to *device-measured* behaviour, d = 0.29. Two direct resistance-training choice trials found perceived autonomy up and enjoyment null. Honour preference because it costs nothing measurable, not because it grows more muscle.

**What is claimed.** That letting a trainee choose their own exercises — and refuse ones they
dislike — improves enjoyment and adherence, and that the adherence gain outweighs whatever
programme quality is lost; therefore a generator should honour preference.

**What the evidence actually shows.**

*The general autonomy claim has meta-analytic support, and it reaches measured behaviour.*
Ntoumanis et al. (2021) pooled 73 SDT-informed health interventions: small-to-medium changes in
motivational constructs, and **g = 0.28 on health behaviour at follow-up**. For physical activity
the split by measurement is the part that matters: d+ = 0.23 self-reported, **d+ = 0.29
device-assessed**. The effect does not shrink when you stop asking people and start measuring
them, which is the failure mode most motivational literature dies of. Mediation ran through need
support and *autonomous* motivation, not controlled motivation.

*But "autonomy support" in those trials is a counselling style, not an exercise picker.* None of
the 73 studies manipulated which exercises a resistance-training programme contained. Applying
d = 0.29 to a checkbox that excludes lateral raises is an extrapolation across at least two
levels of abstraction, and it is the single largest inference in this note.

*The two trials that did manipulate choice inside a resistance session found autonomy up and
enjoyment flat.* Halperin et al. (2020), 17 elite youth athletes, randomised crossover,
self-selected versus predetermined **exercise order**: power trivial-to-small (p >= 0.07,
ES <= 0.21), fatigue null, and **enjoyment p = 0.72, ES = 0.05**. Emanuel et al. (2021), 19
resistance-trained adults, self-selected versus fixed **repetitions** within 8-12: enjoyment 77.9
versus 70.8 on a 100-point scale, **p = 0.40**, most comparisons returning Bayes factors above
2.1 *for* the null — but **perceived autonomy 3.05 versus 2.19, p < 0.001**, and preference for
the condition split almost evenly. **The manipulation worked on the construct it targeted and did
not reach enjoyment.** The corpus should not repeat the "choice makes it more fun" claim.

*The affective findings that are positive come from load, not exercise selection.* The 2024
older-adults RCT (self-selected load, 12 weeks, n=24) found lower RPE and higher affective
valence at weeks 8-12 with equivalent outcomes. Baz-Valle 2019 found varied exercise selection
raised intrinsic motivation with no hypertrophy difference. Both are affect and motivation
outcomes in trials too short and too small to observe adherence.

*Affect is the one construct with a demonstrated forward link to behaviour.* Rhodes & Kates
(2015), 24 studies: positive change in **during-exercise** affect predicted future physical
activity; **post-exercise** affect did not. So the mechanism by which disliking an exercise could
matter is real and has a specific shape — it runs through how a set feels while it is happening.

*What autonomy demonstrably costs, when it is given over load.* Barbosa-Netto et al. (2022), 18
studies, 368 participants: trainees left to pick their own load select **53% of 1RM (95% CrI
49-58%)**, with little moderation by experience, age or sex. This is the clearest measured price
of self-selection anywhere in the literature — and it is a price on *load*, not on *which
exercise*. It is the reason this note does not generalise into "let the user choose everything".

*Base rates, for scale.* Resistance-programme dropout is reported at 22-38% and rises with
programme length; fitness-centre attrition runs roughly 40-65% in the first six months. Whatever
adherence is worth, there is a lot of it available to lose.

**What it does not settle.**

- **No trial has randomised exercise *selection* — chosen versus assigned — with adherence as an
  outcome, in resistance training.** That is the exact question, and it is unasked.
- **Nothing measures whether an excluded exercise would have been skipped anyway.** The premise
  that refusing an exclusion converts it into a silent skip is mechanism, not data.
- **Nothing tells you the exchange rate.** How many fractional sets of lost volume one unit of
  preserved adherence is worth has never been estimated. See
  `references/indirect-only-volume-and-the-coverage-floor.md` for the other half of that
  arithmetic, which is also unpriced.
- **The autonomy effect may be dose-shaped and this literature cannot see the shape.** Whether
  one exclusion and thirty differ is untested; SDT predicts diminishing returns on choice,
  choice-overload literature predicts a reversal, and neither has been run here.
- **Directional risk of the opposite policy is untested.** Controlling-style literature links
  need-thwarting to *intention* to drop out in sport — not to measured attendance, and not in a
  solo lifter with an app.
- **Every affect finding is short-term.** 8-12 weeks, small n, mostly untrained or youth
  athletes. The proposition is about a year, and nothing here observed one.

**A distinction the rest of this corpus does not yet make.** `TD-004` and `TD-015` both record
that deriving preference from logged behaviour beats asking. The evidence for "asking is
unreliable" is about **intentions** — the 47.6% intention-behaviour gap, self-report
overestimating activity. **An exclusion is not an intention.** It is a report of past affect, and
Rhodes & Kates found during-exercise affect is the one construct with a forward link to
behaviour, while intention is the construct that famously lacks one. So "I hate barbell overhead
press" is closer to what predicts than "I intend to train five days a week". **A stated exclusion
is more trustworthy input than a stated ranking**, and the corpus's general scepticism about
asking should not be applied to both equally.

**Provenance caveat.** Ntoumanis' d+ = 0.29 device-assessed figure came through a search summary
rather than the paper; the open-access PDF would confirm it in one fetch. Everything else here
was read from a fetched page.

**Where it touches the product.**

- Supports honouring a stated exclusion **as a default**, on a weak-but-real prior plus a
  near-zero measured cost — not on a growth claim. `TD-015`'s permitted-wording rule applies
  unchanged.
- Argues against the system *silently* doing something the user asked it not to do: the only
  construct the manipulation reliably moves is **perceived autonomy**, which a covert override
  damages most directly.
- Does **not** support extending self-selection to load, repetitions or proximity to failure.
  `TD-009`, `TD-010` and `TD-011` stay prescribed, and the 53%-of-1RM finding is the concrete
  reason. Recorded as `TD-016`.
