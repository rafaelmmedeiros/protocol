**What is claimed.** That different muscle groups need different weekly set volumes — small
muscles (delts, arms, calves) more, large muscles (quads, back) fewer, or some other ordering
depending on who is asserting it.

**What the evidence actually shows.**

*No meta-analysis or meta-regression found stratifies its volume dose-response by muscle group.*
Pelland (2025/2026) models fractional weekly volume against hypertrophy pooled across
measurement sites and reports no muscle-group moderator. ACSM 2026 states one threshold, "per
muscle group," with no differentiation. Schoenfeld's 2019 frequency pool measured only thigh and
arm sites. **The absence is the finding: the volume literature is written as if muscles are
interchangeable, because it has never had the data to say otherwise.**

*The one direct comparison points weakly at a difference, in a surprising direction.* Baz-Valle
et al. (2022), moderate (12-20) versus high (over 20) weekly sets in trained men, found no
difference for quadriceps (p=0.19) or biceps brachii (p=0.59) but a significant advantage for
high volume in **triceps brachii** (p=0.01). That is one p-value from a 7-study review, in the
muscle that receives the largest indirect volume from pressing — a plausible confound rather
than a physiological law about small muscles. It is also the opposite of the usual "small muscles
need less" story.

*What is universally cited instead is practice, not outcome.* A 2025 survey of 154 competitive
physique athletes documents 20-32 weekly sets for chest and back, 12-19 for arms, 14-20 for
calves, 16-24 for quadriceps. This is a description of what a self-selected, heavily
volume-tolerant population does. It is not evidence that those distributions caused their
physiques, and the authors say plainly that no study has examined whether those practices align
with the guidelines.

*Mechanistic arguments for muscle-specific dosing exist and none is tested against a hypertrophy
outcome.* Fibre-type distribution (soleus), habitual daily loading (calves), and recovery
kinetics of small versus large muscles are all invoked in practitioner writing. No trial found
here compares weekly volumes within a muscle group against the same comparison in a different
muscle group, which is what the claim requires.

**What it does not settle.**

- **Whether muscles genuinely differ in dose-response is open, not refuted.** `thin` means thin
  in both directions: this note does not license "all muscles are identical," it licenses
  "nobody has shown otherwise, so do not build a table."
- **The triceps result is unreplicated** and confounded with indirect volume.
- **Calves, side delts, rear delts, forearms and erectors are near-absent** from the pooled
  hypertrophy literature entirely, so any number assigned to them is extrapolation.
- **The indirect/direct distinction may be doing all the work people attribute to muscle size.**
  A muscle receiving 4 fractional sets of which 3 come from 0.5-weighted secondary roles has had
  a different stimulus history than one receiving 4 direct sets, and no source found separates
  these.

**Provenance caveat.** Baz-Valle's triceps p=0.01 was read through a bibliographic aggregator's
rendering of the abstract, not the paper. It is the sole muscle-specific signal found; if it
evaporates this note gets thinner, not different.

**Where it touches the product.**

- **Supports a single uniform weekly fractional target across all modelled muscle groups**
  (`TD-008`), with any differentiation treated as convention.
- **The real per-muscle asymmetry in our system is arithmetic, not physiological.** Under
  `TD-006`, front delts and triceps reach a uniform target largely through 0.5s while side delts
  and calves reach it only through direct slots. That is a selection and coverage problem for the
  generator, not a reason to move the target.
- **It is the note to cite when a future session proposes a per-muscle volume table.** The table
  would be inventing numbers, and `TD-005`'s omission discipline applies.
