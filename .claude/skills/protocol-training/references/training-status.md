**What is claimed.** That "beginner", "intermediate" and "advanced" are meaningful training
categories, that they can be determined from a person, and that a programme should differ by
which one someone is in.

**What the evidence actually shows.**

*The construct is not standardised, and this part is not in dispute.* Buckner et al. (2017)
survey how the literature admits subjects and find three incompatible practices in use: a
minimum training duration, a strength threshold alone, or the two combined. Their conclusion is
negative and blunt — given the spread of individual strength responses, strength cannot separate
trained from untrained people; they fall back on training age plus modality as the least bad
criteria, not as good ones. A 2025 narrative review of elite vs recreational adaptation records
the same problem from the other end: "elite" means national or professional competition in some
studies and more than one year of lifting in others.

*The one published attempt at an objective model is a proposal, not a validated instrument.*
Santos Junior et al. (2021) score five parameters — current uninterrupted training time,
detraining time, previous experience, exercise technique, and strength level — and average them
into four levels (beginner, intermediate, advanced, highly advanced). It exists precisely
because the authors state there are no objective parameters to assess training status. It is
expert consensus published in a practitioner journal; no validation study establishing that its
levels predict response was found. Two of its five inputs (technique, strength level) require
observed performance, and two more are self-reported history.

*Training age predicts current trainability poorly, because individual variance swamps it.*
Hubal et al. (2005), 585 subjects, 12 weeks of identical progressive training in untrained arms:
biceps CSA changed -2% to +59%, 1RM 0% to +250%, isometric strength -32% to +149%. That is the
range *within* a single training status, on a single controlled programme. Any two-to-four
category grouping is coarse relative to that spread. Direction-of-effect by status is real and
consistent — untrained people gain relatively more, and recover more slowly than elite athletes
by a claimed 30-50% (narrative review, weak evidence) — but it is a group mean sitting inside a
much larger individual distribution.

*And the prescriptions barely diverge anyway, for hypertrophy.* This is the finding with the
most bearing and the least attention.

- **ACSM 2009**, the position stand that invented the novice/intermediate/advanced framing for
  most practitioners, prescribes *identical* hypertrophy loading for novice and intermediate:
  70-85% 1RM, 8-12 reps, 1-3 sets per exercise, 1-2 min rest. The two levels differ only in
  frequency and split organisation. Advanced widens to 70-100% 1RM, 1-12 reps, 3-6 sets.
- **ACSM 2026**, its first update in 17 years and built on 137 systematic reviews and 30,000+
  participants, does not differentiate protocols by training status at all. It gives one
  hypertrophy number — roughly 10 sets per muscle group per week, all major muscle groups at
  least twice weekly — and notes only that athletes would need specific plans.
- **Volume dose-response** (Pelland et al. 2025; 67 studies, 2,058 participants): hypertrophy
  rises ~0.24% per additional fractional weekly set at the mean volume of 12.25 sets (95% CrI
  0.15-0.33%), with diminishing returns and no observed plateau. Minimum effective dose ~4
  fractional sets; sets 5-10 give the best return per unit of recovery cost; sets 11-18 return
  roughly 70% of that, 19+ roughly 50%. Baz-Valle et al. (2022), restricted to subjects with
  at least 1 year of experience, lands on 12-20 weekly sets. These two ranges overlap heavily.
- **Rep range and load** are effectively status-independent for hypertrophy: growth is similar
  across roughly 6-35 reps when sets are taken near failure; load matters for strength, not size.
- **Frequency** has a negligible independent effect on hypertrophy once weekly volume is equated
  (Pelland 2025: posterior probability 91.3% positive, credible interval crossing zero).
- **Proximity to failure** is the one variable where a status difference is plausibly
  actionable, and the evidence there is about *measurement* rather than *prescription* — see
  the boundary below, and `references/cold-start-first-block.md`.

**What it does not settle.**

- **Whether the volume dose-response curve itself differs by status.** Pelland et al. included
  28 studies of beginners and 39 of trained lifters but entered training status as a statistical
  adjustment, not as a reported moderator. No meta-analysis found here reports a
  status-stratified volume-hypertrophy curve. The overlap of the ranges is therefore an
  inference from separate analyses, not a tested null.
- **The upper end for trained lifters is genuinely contested.** Aube et al. (2022) found no
  difference between 12, 18 and 24 weekly sets in trained men (squat 2.09x body mass), while
  Baz-Valle's review recommends up to 20 and Pelland's curve never flattens. Competent people
  disagree, and the disagreement is about the region above ~12 sets — a region a first block
  should not be in regardless.
- **Whether "advanced" needs anything the literature has not measured**, e.g. periodisation
  structure, exercise variation, or specialisation blocks. Absence of a status-differentiated
  recommendation in ACSM 2026 is partly absence of evidence: the trials are short and mostly on
  young trained males in their 20s-30s.
- **Nothing here validates self-report.** No study was found measuring the reliability of a
  user's own beginner/intermediate/advanced selection against any objective criterion.
- **Everything above concerns hypertrophy.** Strength shows sharper diminishing returns to
  volume and a real frequency effect; do not carry these conclusions across.

**Provenance caveat.** Some claims above reached this note through secondary summaries rather
than the primary text, because the publisher fetches were blocked when it was written:
Pelland's per-set estimate with its credible interval, and the 28/39 study split with status as
a covariate rather than a moderator (both corroborated across two independent secondary
sources); and the ACSM 2009 by-level recommendation table. The ACSM 2026 claim — the strongest
single support for `ADR-004` here — rests on ACSM's own summary page rather than the full
stand. Treat those as well-attested but unverified at source. A decision may cite them; a
decision that turns on one of them precisely should verify it first.

*Narrowed 2026-08-23 during `S1.2`.* The frequency figure — 91.3% posterior probability, wide
interval — is no longer in this caveat: it was verified against the authors' own discussion
wording, and Pelland is now published rather than a preprint (Sports Medicine 56:481-505). The
citation above points at the published paper. See
`references/per-muscle-training-frequency.md`, which is built on it.

**Where it touches the product.**

- **Confirms `ADR-004`.** The rejected option B assumed level would be measured as "how long
  have you been training", and the ADR's own reasoning — that training age and training status
  are not the same variable — is what the literature says. Stronger than that: even a *correct*
  status label would not change a hypertrophy prescription in the range a first block occupies.
  The cost of omitting the field is smaller than the ADR conservatively assumed.
- **Sets the shape of any future status inference.** If the system ever derives a level from
  logged training, Santos Junior's parameters are the only published template, and its two
  observable inputs (strength level, technique) are the two this system can least easily read
  from Hevy data. What it *can* observe — load and rep progression over time, i.e. rate of
  response — is not in any published classification.
- **Bounds what a decision may assert.** A weekly-set threshold for hypertrophy is defensible
  in the 4-12 range for anyone; a threshold above ~12 sets cannot cite settled evidence, and a
  threshold that varies by user-declared level cannot cite any. `TD-001` is written inside that
  bound.
