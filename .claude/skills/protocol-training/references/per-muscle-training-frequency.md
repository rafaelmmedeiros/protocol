**What is claimed.** That a muscle must be trained some minimum number of times per week to
grow — usually stated as "at least twice" — and that higher per-muscle frequency produces more
hypertrophy than lower.

**What the evidence actually shows.**

*The independent effect of frequency on hypertrophy is not detectable once weekly volume is
held constant.* Four analyses agree, from three research groups, using three different methods:

- **Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger (2019)**, 25 studies, 836 participants, the analysis built
  specifically to isolate frequency: no significant difference between higher and lower
  frequency on a volume-equated basis; the confidence interval crosses zero. The 2022 umbrella
  review rated it high methodological quality (81%) with high GRADE evidence, and adopted its
  conclusion verbatim: frequency "does not significantly or meaningfully impact muscle
  hypertrophy when volume is equated."
- **Pelland et al. (2025/2026)**, 67 studies, 2,058 participants, Bayesian multi-level
  meta-regression with volume and frequency in the same model, adjusted for intervention
  duration and training status. Volume's slope had 100% posterior probability of exceeding
  zero; frequency's did not — 91.3% for the linear slope in the primary hypertrophy
  meta-regression, with a wide uncertainty interval. Their reading of it is that the interval is
  compatible with a negligible effect, not that frequency is proven inert.
- **Ramos-Campo et al. (2024)**, 14 trials, 392 healthy adults aged 18-40, attacking the same
  question from the split side rather than the frequency side: split versus full-body routines
  produced no difference in elbow extensor (MD 0.30, p=0.84), elbow flexor (MD 0.17, p=0.91) or
  vastus lateralis growth (MD -0.08, p=0.93), nor in lean body mass (MD -0.07, p=0.92) or in
  bench press or lower-limb strength. Heterogeneity was I2=0% across the hypertrophy measures.
- **Cuthbert et al. (2021)**, well-trained populations, strength outcome: no clear differences
  between frequencies over 6-12 weeks.

*The apparent 2x effect in the older literature is a volume effect wearing frequency's clothes.*
Schoenfeld's 2016 meta-analysis — the source of the "train each muscle twice a week"
recommendation now embedded in ACSM 2026 and most practitioner writing — pooled 10 studies
comparing 1 versus 2+ sessions per muscle and found roughly 6.8% versus 3.7% growth favouring
higher frequency. Its own stated caveat was that most included studies were not volume-equated,
so the frequency effect could not be separated from the volume effect. The 2019 paper is
Schoenfeld re-running the question under volume-equated inclusion criteria and reaching the
opposite conclusion. **The two are not in conflict and must not be cited as competing
positions: the later one supersedes the earlier one on the narrower question, and the earlier
one remains correct on the wider one** — if you do not control volume, more sessions means more
sets, and more sets means more growth. Anyone citing "Schoenfeld says 2x" is citing the
superseded one.

*Individual trials scatter around the null in both directions, which is why the meta-analyses
matter more than any one of them.* Saric et al. (2019), 27 trained men, 6 weeks, volume-equated:
3 vs 6 sessions per week produced statistically indistinguishable results on every measure
(elbow extensor +14% vs +11%, rectus femoris +5% vs +6%, vastus intermedius +10% vs +11%).
Lasevicius et al. (2019), trained men, 10 weeks, volume-equated 2 vs 3 sessions per muscle: no
significant differences, though every hypertrophy point estimate favoured 3x, some by a wide
margin (elbow extensors +8.6% vs +15.7%) — an underpowered trial pointing at a real effect is
indistinguishable from noise, and this is what a wide credible interval looks like at the trial
level. Zaroni et al. (2019) is the strongest counterexample: 18 trained men, volume-equated,
5x/muscle full-body versus 1x/muscle split, greater muscle thickness gains for the
high-frequency group in elbow flexors and vastus lateralis. n=18 against 67 pooled studies is
not a reason to change the conclusion, but it is a reason not to state the conclusion as
"frequency is irrelevant."

*Frequency is not inert for strength.* Pelland found 100% posterior probability that strength
gains increase with frequency, with diminishing returns — a genuine outcome-dependent split in
the evidence. Cuthbert found no clear difference in well-trained subjects over 6-12 weeks, so
even this is not unanimous. **Our goal is hypertrophy only; do not import the strength finding.**

*What frequency does do is govern per-session volume, and per-session volume has its own curve.*
Remmert et al. (2025), same dataset and method as Pelland: per-session set volume shows a
positive dose-response for hypertrophy (100% posterior probability), with the point of
undetectable outcome superiority at **~11 fractional sets per session per muscle**. Below that
ceiling, cramming a week's volume into fewer sessions costs nothing measurable. Above it, adding
sets to the same session stops paying. This is the mechanism by which very low frequency can
lose: not because the muscle needs stimulating twice, but because a once-weekly session has to
carry all the volume, and past ~11 sets the extra sets are wasted.

*"Sets per muscle" is a fractional count, not a direct one.* Baz-Valle et al. (2022) and both
2025 meta-regressions count an indirect set (biceps on a row, triceps on a press) as 0.5. This
is load-bearing for us: it means the split determines a muscle's weekly volume partly through
exercises that do not name it, and a generator that counts only direct sets will systematically
under-read arm and shoulder volume on any push/pull or upper/lower template.

**What it does not settle.**

- **The null is a wide interval, not a demonstrated zero.** Pelland's own conclusion is that the
  91.3% figure and the wide interval "permit additional study into the potential programming
  configurations (e.g., muscle group trained, training status, proximity to failure) that may
  elicit greater muscle hypertrophy with higher frequencies." Read as "no reason to prefer high
  frequency," not "high frequency cannot help."
- **The per-session ceiling is contested.** Remmert puts it near 11 fractional sets; a
  well-known practitioner-facing analysis (Krieger/Weightology) argues for roughly 6-8 hard sets
  per muscle per session. Remmert is the more recent and more systematic, but the disagreement
  is live and it sits directly on top of the extreme low-frequency case. Nothing in our
  supported range is near either number, so we do not have to choose.
- **Nothing above 3x/muscle/week has good coverage.** Trials at 4x, 5x and 6x per muscle are
  few, small and short, and Zaroni is the only one favouring high frequency.
- **Muscle-group specificity is untested.** Schoenfeld's 2019 pool measured only thigh and arm
  sites. Whether delts, calves or forearms — smaller, faster-recovering, more indirectly loaded
  — behave the same is not established by any of this.
- **Durations are 6-12 weeks, subjects overwhelmingly young men.** A frequency difference that
  needs a year to express itself would not appear anywhere in this literature.
- **It says nothing about adherence.** Every finding here is volume-equated by design, which
  assumes the volume actually gets done. Which frequency a real person completes is a different
  question the literature does not address, and for our single user it may be the only one that
  matters. See `references/cold-start-first-block.md`.

**Where it touches the product.**

- **The split is not a hypertrophy decision.** Given a weekly per-muscle volume, the generator
  may distribute it however scheduling, session length and preference dictate, and cite this
  note. No split arrangement is defensible as "better for growth." Recorded as `TD-003`.
- **The floor is 2x per muscle per week, and it is a soft one.** ACSM 2026 says at least twice
  weekly; the volume-equated evidence does not require it. Adopt it because every sane template
  at 2-6 days/week delivers it anyway and it keeps per-session volume comfortable — not because
  1x is shown to fail.
- **The generator must count fractional sets.** A muscle's weekly volume includes 0.5 per
  indirect set. This is a concrete requirement on the volume accounting, not a nuance, and
  `S1.4` inherits it.
- **Corroborates `TD-001` and `training-status`.** The frequency line in `training-status` is now
  verified at the source, and Pelland's model adjusted for training status while finding it.
