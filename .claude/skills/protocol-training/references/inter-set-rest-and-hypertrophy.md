**What is claimed.** That short inter-set rest (~60 s) enhances hypertrophy through metabolic
stress, or — in the modern reversal — that longer rest (2-3 min or more) is superior; and that
heavy compound exercises require longer rest than isolation exercises.

**What the evidence actually shows.**

*The direct hypertrophy evidence is thin and the effect is small.* The 2024 Bayesian systematic
review pooled **9 studies, 184 participants, 19 measurement points** (thigh 10, arm 6, whole
body 3), comparing 60 s or less against more than 60 s. Controlled effect sizes: **arm 0.13
(95% CrI -0.27 to 0.51), thigh 0.17 (-0.13 to 0.43), whole body -0.08 (-0.45 to 0.29)**. Every
interval crosses zero. The authors' conclusion is "a small benefit to employing longer versus
shorter inter-set rest intervals" and that existing short-rest guidelines "warrant
reconsideration." In an untrained subanalysis the quadriceps effect was 0.17 and arm/whole-body
were negligible (0.02 and -0.05). Proximity to failure did not moderate the result.

*The trial the practitioner world quotes is small.* Schoenfeld et al. (2016): **21** trained men,
8 weeks, 1-minute versus 3-minute rest, favouring 3 minutes for triceps and quadriceps thickness
and for 1RM strength. It is one study of 21 people and roughly a fifth of the pooled evidence
above. Grgic et al.'s 2017 review of the same literature called the findings equivocal and
suggested longer rest may matter more for those with training experience.

*What rest reliably determines is the next set's repetitions, and the useful step is 1 to 2
minutes.* Schoenfeld/Haun (2023), Smith-machine back squat and leg extension at 1, 2 and 3
minutes: the **largest difference in repetition performance was between 1 minute and 2-3 minutes,
with trivial differences between 2 and 3 minutes — for both the single-joint and the multi-joint
exercise.** Discomfort was higher for the squat than the leg extension, and lower with longer
rest, in both. Earlier acute work agrees on direction with more spread: Senna et al. (2011) found
3 and 5 minutes beat 1 minute on bench press with no 3-vs-5 difference, and graded differences
across all three intervals on leg press, chest fly and leg extension; a triceps pull-down study
(n=16) found 1 < 3 < 5 minutes at 50% 1RM and no 3-vs-5 difference at 80% 1RM.

*So the "compounds need more rest" convention is only half-supported.* The exercise that suffers
most from short rest in the best-controlled comparison was **not clearly the compound** — the
1-minute penalty appeared in both, and the single-joint exercise at a *light load* was the
condition most sensitive to small increases in rest. What does differ by exercise is perceived
discomfort and metabolic disturbance, which were greater for the squat. Rest scaling by movement
demand is therefore defensible on discomfort and on load magnitude — **not** on a demonstrated
repetition-performance asymmetry between compound and isolation.

**What it does not settle.**

- **Whether rest affects hypertrophy at all, independently of the volume it permits.** The
  cleanest reading is that rest matters because short rest costs reps and load, and reps and load
  are the actual stimulus. No source separates these mechanisms.
- **Anything above 3 minutes.** No hypertrophy evidence found beyond ~3 min; longer is untested,
  not disproven.
- **Whether rest should vary by position in the session.** Nothing found tests within-session
  rest gradients. The idea that later slots need less rest is entirely inference from lighter
  loads and lower joint demand, and it cuts against the fact that fatigue is *higher* later.
- **Whether rest varies usefully by laterality.** Unilateral work provides contralateral rest by
  construction; no hypertrophy study found addresses it.
- **The trade against session duration and adherence is unstudied.** Rest is the single largest
  determinant of session length, and no source found weighs longer sessions against completion.
- **9 studies, 184 people, mostly young, mixed training status.** One of the thinnest evidence
  bases in this corpus for a variable every programme must specify.

**Provenance caveat.** The 2024 Bayesian review is cited by title and journal without an author
list, which could not be confirmed. Complete the citation before anything turns on its
authorship.

**Where it touches the product.**

- **Rest is prescribable per slot without harm**, and a per-slot value is better justified than a
  single session-wide value — but the justification is discomfort and load magnitude, not growth.
  Recorded as `TD-011`.
- **It sets a defensible floor at about 90 seconds.** The one consistent finding across the acute
  literature is that 60 seconds costs repetitions in both compound and isolation exercises. **A
  60-second slot is not supported by this evidence**, even at the end of a session.
- **The 2-versus-3-minute choice is free.** Nothing distinguishes them for repetitions or growth;
  the difference is session time, which is `S1.5`'s constraint, not this note's.
- **It makes rest the main lever on session duration.** Because the evidence between 90 s and
  180 s is essentially flat, rest is the *cheapest* variable to trim against a time budget —
  cheaper than cutting a set, which moves a muscle down the volume curve.
